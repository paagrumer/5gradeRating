Component Stars

Each ECU earns a star score from its confirmed vulnerabilities, following Algorithm 1. Select any component to see how its value is derived. This step yields the star score alone. A clean ECU scores a full 5.00, and any vulnerability caps it at 4.75 under the 5% residual rule. The weighting and the aggregation follow in Vehicle Stars. The rules are set out in the Technical description tab, and the tests that check them in Verification & validation.

Vehicle Stars

Each star score is weighted by Table H.8 and combined into a single rating, R = Σ(s·w) / Σ(w). The weight has two parts. The impact follows from the component's ASIL, and the feasibility follows from the CVSS exploitability of its findings, raised where the component communicates widely or handles personal data. The rules are given in the Technical description tab, and the tests in Verification & validation.

How the rating is worked out, step by step

The full calculation for this vehicle, from start to finish, with the standard behind each step. Select any component above to see its own derivation.

  1. Collect the confirmed vulnerabilities for each ECU. Each is a public CVE with a CVSS v3.1 base score and vector. NVD · CVSS v3.1 (FIRST)
  2. Component Stars, the star score s (Algorithm 1). Group the findings by category, retain the worst CVSS in each category to prevent dilution, and take the three worst. These are then weighted by their count. One category uses 1·C₀. Two use 0.6·C₀ + 0.4·C₁. Three use 0.6·C₀ + 0.3·C₁ + 0.1·C₂. This yields the component's combined score CVSS_C, and then s = −0.725·CVSS_C + 5. A clean ECU scores a full 5.00. Any vulnerability caps the star score at 4.75, which expresses the 5% residual insecurity that cannot be certified away. CVSS_C is the value that Algorithm 1 in CSCS'23 denotes X. Paper Algorithm 1 · CSCS'23
  3. Impact (the H.8 row) follows from the ASIL, so QM is Negligible and D is Severe. Where the component handles personal data, it rises by one level. ISO 26262-3 · GDPR Art. 35
  4. Feasibility (the H.8 column) follows from the CVSS exploitability E = 8.22·AV·AC·PR·UI of those same worst categories, combined with the same weights used for the star score, then mapped to a Table G.8 band. Where the component communicates across domains it rises (E-D adds one, E-C adds two). ISO/SAE 21434 Annex G.3 + G.8 · CVSS v3.1
  5. Weight w is just the cell you land on in Table H.8, a number from 1 to 5. ISO/SAE 21434 Annex H
  6. Vehicle Stars, worked out as R = Σ(sᵢ·wᵢ) / Σ(wᵢ) over the components that matter for security. Anything critical (CVSS ≥ 7) gets flagged and pulled out rather than hidden in an average. For this car you end up with R = 3.65. ISO/SAE 21434 · UN ECE R155 §7.2.2.2

In one paragraph

The whole method in plain terms, before the detail below.

The tool assigns a vehicle a cybersecurity rating on a five-star scale, in the spirit of the NCAP crash-safety score. It proceeds in two steps. Each ECU is first scored from the vulnerabilities confirmed in it, with the most severe carrying the most weight, which gives its Component Stars. Those component scores are then combined into a single rating for the vehicle, weighted towards the components that matter most, which gives the Vehicle Stars. Every value traces to a public standard, so the result can be independently checked. CVSS establishes the severity of a vulnerability, and ISO 26262 sets the importance of a component through its ASIL. Table H.8 of ISO/SAE 21434 combines the two.

Scope of this work

What the base pipeline establishes now, and what is deliberate future work.

In scope (base pipeline)
The full rating, end to end: a component star score (the three worst finding categories, CSCS Algorithm 1) → a Table H.8 weight (how much the component matters × how feasible an attack is) → the vehicle rating R = Σ(s·w)/Σ(w), plus a repeatable simulation to study whole fleets. Every rule rests on a standard.
Why CVSS (not the attack-potential method)
ISO/SAE 21434 Annex G allows three ways to judge attack feasibility. This work uses CVSS. The reason is practical: CVSS vectors are machine-readable and link straight to the NVD, so the same vectors the simulator produces can feed a vulnerability database for automatic penetration testing. The attack-potential method needs an expert panel and fits no scanner.
Future work (out of scope now)
Linking the finding categories to the UN R155 Annex 5 threat list, adding a weakest-link cap next to the weighted mean, and calibrating the feature weights with industry data. These are identified, not built, the goal here is to prove the base model works.

Architecture

The vehicle E/E model and its three paradigms.

What
Represents the car as functional domains, physical zones and ECUs, and draws the Distributed → Domain → Zonal evolution from the same roster.
Roster
The 18 cybersecurity-relevant components of Table 2 (not-CS-relevant rows omitted). Each ECU is anonymised and tags its source: the three real components are Main ADAS Controller, Battery Controller, and the Central Infotainment Head Unit (publicly disclosed CVEs, each linked to the NVD in Component Stars). The rest are simulated. Every ECU keeps a ref back to its Table 2 row.
Vulnerability treatment
The rating is a point-in-time residual-risk view. Consistent with ISO/SAE 21434 risk treatment and the UN R155 CSMS, any disclosed finding scoring ≥ 7.0 (High/Critical) is taken to have been reported and mitigated by the supplier through coordinated disclosure, and is therefore excluded from the assessed set, the same threshold as the model's critical circuit-breaker. Findings below 7.0 are carried as accepted residual risk and drive the star score. On the real head unit this excludes CVE-2023-34399 (9.8), CVE-2023-34402 (7.7) and the 7.5-rated set (CVE-2023-34397/34398/34400) as mitigated, leaving the sub-7 findings as residual.

Vehicle · Weight (Table H.8)

Impact (row)
From the component ASIL via asilToImpact (QM→Negligible, A→Moderate, B→Major, C/D→Severe). If a PIA confirms the component processes PII, the impact is promoted one level (e.g. Moderate→Major), capped at Severe, the binary privacy column-shift.
Feasibility (column)
The CVSS exploitability E = 8.22·V·C·P·U (Annex G.3) of the same worst categories the star score uses, combined with the same case weights (case 2: 0.6·E₀ + 0.4·E₁, and so on). So both Table H.8 axes react to a component the same way, which keeps it objective. That value maps to a Table G.8 band. A clean component sits at the lowest band. Then network interaction raises it: same-domain +0, cross-domain +1, connectivity +2, capped at High.
Weight
w = H.8[impact][feasibility], an integer 1–5, capped at 5. This is the Table H.8 risk-matrix value. It reproduces the Table H.9 worked examples (Spoofing: Severe & High→5. DoS: Moderate & Low→2), cross-checked independently by the Table H.10 formula R = 1 + I×F.
Standards
ISO 26262-3 ASIL · ISO/SAE 21434 Annex G.3 (CVSS exploitability) · Table G.8 · Annex H Tables H.8/H.9/H.10.
Feasibility decisions
Each finding stores a published CVSS base score (from the disclosed vulnerability list) that drives the star score, plus an exploitability vector (AV/AC/PR/UI) that drives feasibility. For real NVD-enriched CVEs these coincide. For the anonymised head-unit CVEs, whose base scores are published but whose v3.1 vectors NVD has not enriched, the vector reflects the vendor-stated physical/USB access (AV:P → Very Low feasibility), documented per finding in config.js. Scope-changed PR is not re-scaled, consistent with the G.3 range P∈[0.27, 0.85]. Clean components take the lowest feasibility base. Switching them to their inherent exposure is a one-line change.

Vehicle · Rating

Aggregation
R = Σ(sᵢ·wᵢ) / Σ(wᵢ) over the cybersecurity-relevant components, the weighted arithmetic mean of the star scores. Not-cs-relevant components are excluded. A component flagged CRITICAL at the component level is reported and held out of the mean.
Properties
All star scores 5 → R = 5 exactly. A finding lowers R in proportion to that component's weight, so the same vulnerability on a network-exposed ASIL D ECU (w=5) moves R more than on an isolated QM controller (w=1). Because the denominator is the sum of weights, the result is not diluted by the number of low-weight components.
Reference fleet
The 18-component roster gives Σ(s·w)=116.72 / Σw=32 → R = 3.65 → ★★★⯪ (3 full + 1 half). The rating is reported to the hundredth exactly as computed on a [0, 5] scale, so there is no rounding and no subjectivity in the number. The star icon is only a display aid drawn to the nearest half-star. A 5.00 means zero vulnerabilities. The moment a component has any vulnerability its star score is capped at 4.75, because we cannot certify more than 95% security.

Component · Star score

Categories
Findings are first grouped by category (Networks, Diagnostics, Software, Cryptography, …) and only the worst CVSS in each category is kept. This is the anti-dilution step: many findings in one area cannot pad or drag the score, one value represents each category. Only the three worst categories then count (c₁ ≥ c₂ ≥ c₃).
Three branches
The kept category severities C₀ ≥ C₁ ≥ C₂ (worst first) run through the threshold branches of Algorithm 1:
• All categories < 5.3 → weighted by count: case 1: X = 1.0·C₀ · case 2: X = 0.6·C₀ + 0.4·C₁ · case 3: X = 0.6·C₀ + 0.3·C₁ + 0.1·C₂ · case 0 (no vulns) → X = 0.
• All < 7 but at least one ≥ 5.3 → Worst-case (≥ 5.3 · single vuln used): X = 1.0·C₀, only the single worst category counts. The lesser ones are not blended in.
• Any category ≥ 7 (High/Critical) → Error: "critical security risk detected", no numeric star score is issued.
Then s = −0.725·X + 5, clamped to [0, 5]. No finding → 5.
Why categories
Grouping by category and keeping only the worst CVSS per category (then capping at three) is the anti-dilution rule: many findings in one area cannot pad or drag the score. Example: the Door Control Module has one finding, so it is one category → case 1 → s = 1.81. Body Control has three findings in three different categories → case 3. Category assignments live in config.js.
Cross-check
Reproduces the paper's Algorithm 2 exactly: C = [5, 4, 3] → X = 0.6·5 + 0.3·4 + 0.1·3 = 4.5 → s = −0.725·4.5 + 5 = 1.74.
Standards
ACM CSCS '23 Algorithm 1 & 2 (5-star-rate) · CVSS base scores.

CVSS base approach, v3.1 (how the values are defined)

Two quantities
Both come from the CVSS Base metric group only (CVSS-B, no Temporal/Threat or Environmental modifiers). (1) The base score (0–10) gives the CVSS severity and feeds the component star score. (2) The exploitability sub-score E = 8.22·V·C·P·U feeds the feasibility via Table G.8. Impact (C/I/A) is not used for feasibility, that axis is handled by ASIL.
How E is calculated
Per ISO/SAE 21434 §G.3 ≡ FIRST CVSS v3.1 §7.1, with V=AV, C=AC, P=PR, U=UI. Worked example, CVE-2019-18827 (AV:N/AC:H/PR:N/UI:N): E = 8.22 × 0.85(AV:N) × 0.44(AC:H) × 0.85(PR:N) × 0.85(UI:N) = 2.22 → Table G.8 → Medium feasibility. The full metric-value derivation:

Decisions & limitations (consolidated, for review)

H.8 is an example
ISO/SAE 21434 presents Tables H.8/H.9/H.10 as example risk matrices, the standard permits organisation-defined matrices. This work adopts the standard's own example verbatim, which is the least subjective choice available. A substitute matrix drops in via config.scoring.h8 without code change.
Star score range
The star score lives on [0, 5] and is reported to the hundredth, which keeps the number objective. A clean component scores exactly 5.00. Any vulnerability caps it at 4.75, since more than 95% security cannot be certified once a vulnerability exists. This 5% residual insecurity is a deliberate rule.
Multi-vuln feasibility = average
With k vulnerabilities, Ē = ΣEᵢ/k per the defined rule. Note the averaging can place a component with one Medium and one Very Low exploit into Low, a deliberate, documented choice (the alternative, worst-of, is a one-line change) that reviewers may probe.
Clean-component feasibility
No confirmed finding → lowest band (no demonstrated exploit), then the interaction shift applies. The alternative basis (inherent exposure) is one line in config.
Feature weights
The security-feature weights are the previous simulator's illustrative assumptions. They shape a simulation input only, and require industry calibration before real-world claims.
Generation scope
Synthetic vulnerabilities are scope-Unchanged v3.1 vectors capped at 6.9 (so the critical branch is never diluted into a mean). Real scope-Changed CVEs are fully supported in assessment (e.g. CVE-2017-5579). ≥7 findings deliberately halt with the critical error rather than score.
Pending
The Distributed/Domain paradigm journal citation is a placeholder in config.paradigms. Verify exact ISO/SAE 21434 clause and table numbers against a controlled copy of the standard before publication.

Simulation

What it does
Generates synthetic vehicles and runs each through the same component + vehicle pipeline as the live reference vehicle (via VRA.vehicle.rateSet, no logic is duplicated). Reports the rating distribution, both Table H.8 axes, the CVSS picture, and exports the data per vehicle and per component.
Configurable
Number of vehicles (1→∞, presets 1000/5000/10000), ECUs per vehicle (0–100), vulnerability probability (manual 0–100%, or derived from security features, fixed or random per vehicle. The 0.05 floor applies only to feature-derived modes, encoding "no real system is fully secure"), Min and Max CVSS of generated vulnerabilities (≤6.9), findings per affected ECU (1–3), PIA probability, and the seed. The domain mix, per-domain ASIL distribution and per-domain network-interaction mix are all editable in the Distributions (advanced) panel (relative weights, normalised per row. A Reset restores the paper's defaults), so every variant of the fleet can be configured without touching the code.
The 0% case
Setting vulnerability probability to 0% yields exactly 5 stars for every vehicle, no finding → full star score (5) → R = Σ(5·w)/Σ(w) = 5 regardless of weights. The manual 0% input is honoured, never silently floored.
Boundary correctness
CVSS v3.1 base scores are discrete (achievable positive scores: 1.6–9.8). The generator enumerates all 1296 scope-U vectors and samples only those inside [Min, Max], boundaries are honoured exactly. An unachievable range is reported in the UI (never silently mis-generated), and inverted Min/Max inputs are normalised. This replaces the earlier rejection sampler, which could emit out-of-range vectors on narrow ranges.
Feasibility-faithful generation
Feasibility depends only on the exploitability E = 8.22·V·C·P·U (AV/AC/PR/UI). The base score also folds in impact (C/I/A). Sampling uniformly over full vectors skews E low, high-E vectors become rare once the base is capped, so feasibility would never leave Very Low/Low on its own. The pool is therefore grouped by exploitability vector: each draw picks a group first (so E is representative across all four Table G.8 bands) and then an impact within it (so the base stays in the severity range). Both axes remain real v3.1 metrics, so the component (02) and vehicle (03) feasibility logic is reproduced exactly and every generated E lands in the ISO range 0.12–3.89.
Security features
Ported catalogue (Secure Boot, HSM EVITA Full/Medium, SecOC, JTAG locks, UDS 0x27/0x29, IDS/IPS, firewalls, fleet learning…): probability starts at 1.00 (unsecured), each enabled feature subtracts its weight, grouped features are mutually exclusive, floor 0.05. Weights are illustrative assumptions pending industry calibration, they set a simulation input only and touch no standard formula. Random per-vehicle selection is seeded, so even "auto" fleets replicate exactly.
Exports
CSV / vehicle, one row per vehicle (seed, vuln probability, class counts, Σsw, Σw, R, stars). CSV / component, the raw generated data (seed, vehicle, domain, ASIL, PIA, interaction, findings, worst CVSS, Ē, feasibility, impact, w, s), kept up to 200 000 components.
Scalability
0–100 ECUs per vehicle spans yesterday's distributed fleets (many small ECUs), today's domain topologies, and tomorrow's zonal/HPC vehicles (fewer, larger). Same rules, any era.
Reproducibility
One seeded PRNG (mulberry32) drives all randomness, including random feature selection. Same seed + config → byte-identical fleet. The seed is written into every CSV.
The charts
Vehicle rating distribution, CVSS base-score distribution by severity, Scalability (mean rating as the ECU count grows from 1 to 100, showing the rules hold from a small car to a big one), Weight sensitivity (how little R moves when the weights are perturbed by ±20%), Weight distribution (the Table H.8 weight per component), and the Populated Table H.8 heatmap (impact by feasibility). The weight scale runs green (low risk) to red (high) for severity, tuned so the ends stay apart for colourblind viewers and in greyscale, and every cell carries its weight number as a second cue. Star distribution and case coverage show as bars. You can export per vehicle, per component, or as chart data.

What the rating means

A plain-language view, no cybersecurity background needed.

The idea

A safety star for cybersecurity

The result is a rating from 0 to 5 stars, reported to two decimal places. More stars mean better protection. The method rates the ECUs in a vehicle, such as braking, engine, doors and display, and combines them into that single figure.

Where it comes from

Grounded in published standards

Each ECU is assessed for known vulnerabilities. The findings are scored against international standards: ISO/SAE 21434 for cybersecurity risk, ISO 26262 for the ASIL, and CVSS for vulnerability severity. The rating supports the UN ECE R155 type-approval process.

Built to scale

One method across vehicle generations

The method applies to any vehicle generation, whether an older car with dozens of ECUs or a next-generation software-defined vehicle. Every simulated fleet is repeatable, so the same seed yields the same result and findings can be independently verified.

Two views

The average and the weakest component

The headline rating is an average that weights the more important components more heavily. The weakest important component is also reported on its own, so a single poor component cannot be masked by the average. Formalising this as a cap is planned future work.

Why trust it

Every rule is auto-checked

The method's rules are tested against known-good values every time it runs, and it reproduces a published worked example exactly. Nothing is hand-waved.

Freshness

A dated snapshot

The rating is true on its test date. A software update or a newly found weakness triggers a re-check, like renewing a safety rating (UN R156 / R155).

What it is not

Not a guarantee

Top marks means no critical weakness was found in the tested set on that date, a bounded, checkable claim, not a promise the car can never be hacked.
